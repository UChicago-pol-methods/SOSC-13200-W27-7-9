## Questions

1. **(2 points)**
   For Butler and Broockman (2011), identify the unit receiving treatment, both manipulated features of the email, the outcome and response deadline, and the characteristics used for blocking.
   Explain why the experiment can estimate an average effect of the alias but cannot establish that a particular nonresponding office discriminated.

Questions 2-3 use this hypothetical blocked experiment, separate from Butler and Broockman's study.
There are two blocks of three offices.
Within each block, exactly one office receives an email signed Jake Mueller and the other two receive an otherwise identical email signed DeShawn Jackson.
The two choices are made independently and uniformly.
All emails omit a partisan signal.
Reply is 1 for a reply and 0 otherwise.

| Office | Block | Observed alias | Observed reply |
|---|---|---|---:|
| A1 | A | Jake | 1 |
| A2 | A | DeShawn | 0 |
| A3 | A | DeShawn | 0 |
| B1 | B | Jake | 1 |
| B2 | B | DeShawn | 1 |
| B3 | B | DeShawn | 0 |

Let $T$ be the mean reply under the Jake label minus the mean reply under the DeShawn label.
For Questions 2-3, the sharp null is that changing the alias would leave every office's reply unchanged.

2. **(3 points)**
   Calculate the observed $T$ in proportions and percentage points.
   Using Gerber and Green (2012), Chapter 3, explain why the sharp null allows you to hold all six replies fixed.
   Enumerate the nine allowed assignments in a table showing the Jake office in each block, its assignment probability, and $T$.
   Compute the exact two-sided p-value, counting $|T|$ at least as large as the observed value.
   State a 5% testing decision and explain it using the null distribution.
