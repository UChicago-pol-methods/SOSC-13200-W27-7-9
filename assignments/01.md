## Case

Questions 1-4 use a hypothetical version of Holland's arithmetic-program example.
Four fourth-grade students receive either a new year-long arithmetic program, $W=1$, or the standard program, $W=0$.
Principals choose each student's program **using whatever criteria they want.**
The outcome is an end-of-year test score from 0 to 10.
Let $Y_i(1)$ and $Y_i(0)$ denote student $i$'s end-of-year scores under the two programs.

| Student | Program received | Observed end-of-year score |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 1 | 8 |
| B | 1 | 6 |
| C | 0 | 5 |
| D | 0 | 3 |

## Questions

1. **(2 points)**
   Identify the unit, the two causes, the response, and the population over which you will average.
   Tabulate $Y_i(0)$ and $Y_i(1)$ for all four students, marking missing outcomes with a ?.
   Write the four-student average causal effect in potential-outcome notation.
   Calculate the observed difference in mean scores between programs.
   Explain whether this identifies the average causal effect, using Holland (1986, Section 3) and/or Tabarrok (2021).

2. **(2 points)**
   Complete two potential-outcome tables with individual effects for all four students: one where every effect is +2 points and one where every effect is -2 points.
   Keep observed scores fixed and all potential scores between 0 and 10.
   Calculate the average causal effect in each table.
   Using Holland (1986, Section 4.4), explain what your tables show about whether constant effects make the observed comparison causal.

3. **(2 points)**
   Suppose a lottery instead assigns two students to each program.
   Using Holland (1986, Section 4.3), explain how this changes the justification for comparing group means.
   Would every allocation produce a difference in means equal to the four-student average causal effect?
   Distinguish an average over repeated lotteries from one lottery's result.
